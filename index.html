About

“We are all made from stardust.” I heard that as a kid and decided it meant I had magical properties. The real version turned out to be better: the heavy elements around us were forged in stars, and working out how they absorb light is now my job.

I'm a PhD candidate in physics at The Ohio State University, based in the Department of Astronomy and the Center for Cosmology and AstroParticle Physics (CCAPP). I work with Prof. Anil K. Pradhan and Prof. Sultana N. Nahar as part of the R-Matrix Opacity Project (RMOP).

I started in astronomy at UBC, spent several years in condensed matter labs growing and measuring quantum materials, and did my honours thesis using machine learning to simulate the Hubbard model. Then I found my way back to the stars.

Research

I work on the atomic physics behind stellar and laboratory opacities. Opacity sets how fast radiation carries energy through a hot, dense plasma, so errors in the atomic data change the temperature structure of a star. The light you see left the Sun's surface about eight minutes ago, but the energy in it may have spent tens of thousands of years working its way out of the core. My work is part of the R-Matrix Opacity Project (RMOP), which revisits the original Opacity Project calculations with larger and more complete atomic models.

The motivation is the solar opacity problem. Solar models built with updated chemical abundances disagree with helioseismology, and laboratory opacity measurements at facilities such as Sandia's Z machine have found higher opacities than theory predicts for some elements. Better atomic data is one way to test whether the theory is the problem.

Breit-Pauli R-matrix calculations

I compute photoionization and photoabsorption cross sections with the Breit-Pauli R-matrix (BPRM) method, using close-coupling expansions with many target states and relativistic fine structure. These calculations resolve the dense series of autoionizing resonances and cover wide photon-energy ranges, structure that is often missing or truncated in earlier opacity data.

Plasma broadening

Resonances in a real plasma are not sharp. I apply a post-processing treatment for the broadening of autoionizing resonances by electron collisions, ion microfields (Stark), thermal Doppler motion and free-free transitions, convolved with the cross sections at each temperature and electron density. This maps out where broadening starts to matter and where resonances dissolve into the continuum.

Opacity and equation of state

The broadened cross sections feed monochromatic and Rosseland mean opacities on temperature-density grids, for single elements and for full stellar mixtures, at conditions found at the base of the solar convection zone and in laboratory experiments. I compare the results with Opacity Project data, and with equation-of-state results in collaboration with an outside group, since both depend on the same plasma conditions.

Computation and tools

Much of the work is code: large atomic physics programs run on high-performance computing clusters, with Python for analysis and automation. I also develop ssopt, a package that automates atomic structure calculations with the SUPERSTRUCTURE code, so that the atomic data feeding these opacities can be produced reproducibly for any ion.

Code

ssopt

A Python package that automates atomic structure calculations with the SUPERSTRUCTURE code. Given the name of an ion, it builds the configuration list from NIST level data, limited by orbital caps (n up to 4 and l up to 3 by default), plus physics rules for electron correlation, then tunes the scaling parameters against NIST energy levels using a surrogate model. It then checks transition rates against NIST and NORAD and exports tables in NORAD format. No hard-coded settings for individual ions, so it works for any ion.

By D. Chari, S. N. Nahar and A. K. Pradhan. Python, Fortran, Slurm. License: CC BY-NC 4.0.
