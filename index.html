About

“We are all made from stardust.” I heard that as a kid and decided it meant I had magical properties. The real version turned out to be better. The heavy elements around us were forged in stars. Working out how they absorb light is now my job.

I'm a PhD candidate in physics at The Ohio State University. I'm based in the Department of Astronomy and the Center for Cosmology and AstroParticle Physics (CCAPP). I work with Prof. Anil K. Pradhan and Prof. Sultana N. Nahar on the R-Matrix Opacity Project (RMOP).

I started in astronomy at UBC. I then spent several years in condensed matter labs, growing and measuring quantum materials. My honours thesis used machine learning to simulate the Hubbard model. Then I found my way back to the stars.

Research

I work on the atomic physics behind stellar and laboratory opacities. Opacity sets how fast radiation moves through a hot, dense plasma. Errors in the atomic data change the temperature structure of a star.

The light you see left the Sun about eight minutes ago. The energy in it spent tens of thousands of years leaving the core. My work is part of the R-Matrix Opacity Project (RMOP). It revisits the original Opacity Project calculations with larger and more complete atomic models.

The motivation is the solar opacity problem. Solar models built with updated abundances disagree with helioseismology. Experiments at Sandia's Z machine measured higher opacities than theory predicts for some elements. Better atomic data helps test whether the theory is at fault.

Breit-Pauli R-matrix calculations

I compute photoionization cross sections with the Breit-Pauli R-matrix (BPRM) method. The calculations include many target states and relativistic fine structure. They resolve dense series of autoionizing resonances over wide photon-energy ranges. Earlier opacity data often miss this structure.

Plasma broadening

Resonances in a real plasma are not sharp. I treat their broadening in post-processing. The treatment includes electron collisions, ion microfields (Stark), Doppler motion and free-free transitions. It is applied at each temperature and electron density. This shows where broadening starts to matter; at high density the resonances dissolve into the continuum.

Opacity and equation of state

The broadened cross sections give monochromatic and Rosseland mean opacities. I compute them on temperature-density grids for single elements and for stellar mixtures. The conditions match the base of the solar convection zone and laboratory experiments. I compare the results with Opacity Project data. I also compare them with equation-of-state results from an outside group; both depend on the same plasma conditions.

Computation and tools

Much of the work is code. Large atomic physics programs run on high-performance computing clusters. I use Python for analysis and automation. I also develop ssopt, a package that automates atomic structure calculations with the SUPERSTRUCTURE code. It produces the atomic data for these opacities reproducibly for any ion.

Code

ssopt

A Python package that automates atomic structure calculations with the SUPERSTRUCTURE code. Give it the name of an ion and it runs the full workflow:

  • Builds the configuration list from NIST level data, with orbital caps (n up to 4 and l up to 3 by default) and physics rules for electron correlation.
  • Tunes the scaling parameters against NIST energy levels using a surrogate model.
  • Checks transition rates against NIST and NORAD.
  • Exports level and transition tables in NORAD format.

There are no hard-coded settings for individual ions, so it works for any ion.

By D. Chari, S. N. Nahar and A. K. Pradhan. Python, Fortran, Slurm. License: CC BY-NC 4.0.
